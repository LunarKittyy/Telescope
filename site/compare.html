How Telescope compares

DroidCam, Iriun and Camo each keep something for their paid version, like HD, manual controls or a picture without a watermark. In Telescope all of it is free. The table goes by each app's own website and store pages, linked at the bottom.

Where Telescope is different

  • Every lens. Pick the ultra-wide, main or telephoto sensor yourself. Zooming in hops over to the telephoto once the framing fits, which is why it gets sharper instead of blurrier.
  • Full manual control of ISO, shutter, focus, white balance and noise reduction, live from the desktop.
  • As much or as little as you want. Most of the desktop app is plugins. Switch off what you don't need and what's left is a webcam that handles exposure and focus on auto and can start whenever an app opens it. Or, if you like to tinker, keep it all on, with auto framing, chroma key, colour grading and the rest, and write a plugin of your own in Python when something's missing.
  • Up to four angles at once from phones, tablets or laptops, each showing up as its own webcam.
  • No account, no cloud, no tracking in the apps. Video goes straight from phone to computer, encrypted. The apps only go online to check GitHub for updates, or to fetch adb or VB-Cable when you ask.
  • Made on Linux. The phone's mic shows up as Telescope Microphone through PulseAudio or PipeWire, with nothing extra to install on most desktops.

Where the others win

  • iPhone app and macOS. Telescope's phone app is Android only and the desktop app runs on Windows and Linux. On an iPhone, Browser camera works with no install, but a native app does more.
  • Tested on more phones. They've been around for years and an unusual phone is more likely to work first time with them.
